What changed for 2026–27

Several rules changed this year, and most families don’t hear about them until an award letter arrives.

ChangeWhat it means for your family
Parent PLUS limitsParents who first borrow on or after July 1, 2026 can borrow at most $20,000 per student per year and $65,000 per student in total. Borrowing the full $20,000 each year hits the total before the fourth year. Northwestern
Parent PLUS cost and repayment9.07% fixed for 2026–27, plus a 4.228% fee taken out of each loan. New Parent PLUS loans can only be repaid on the Tiered Standard plan: no income-based plan and no Public Service Loan Forgiveness. Union College, Credible (NASFAA table)

How families actually pay for college

Families who pay for college almost always combine several sources. For the 2025–26 school year, here is how the average family covered the bill (Sallie Mae and Ipsos survey of 1,000 college students and 1,000 parents, spring 2026):

Parents’ income and savings 39%Scholarships 15%Grants 12%Student borrowing 11%Parent borrowing 11%Student’s income and savings 10%Family and friends 2%

Sallie Mae, How America Pays for College 2026. Sallie Mae is a private student lender.

A few things make that possible:

  • Few families pay the sticker price. Private colleges gave back about 57 cents in their own grants for every dollar of tuition charged to first-year students in 2025–26, and about 9 in 10 first-years received some (NACUBO, via Higher Ed Dive). Compare net prices, not published prices. What It Costs shows the pieces.
  • In-state and WUE tuition cut the biggest line. At CU Boulder, a Colorado student pays $17,513 in tuition and fees, and a student from another state pays $47,142 (2025–26).
  • Paying from three places. A common planning idea: about a third from savings made before college, a third from income during the college years, and a third from loans repaid after, with grants and scholarships lowering the total first (Saving for College).

Rules of thumb for borrowing

GuidelineWhat it means
Student debt under one year’s payTotal student loans at graduation should be less than the expected first-year salary. Then the loans can usually be repaid in 10 years or less. Mark Kantrowitz and Betsy Mayotte, via CNBC
Loan payment under 10% of payA monthly payment around 10% of gross monthly pay is manageable; above 15% is likely too much. Fox Business
Parent debt under one year’s incomeParents’ total college borrowing, for all their children together, should be less than one year of their income, and less if retirement is within 10 years. Mark Kantrowitz
Rent under 30% of incomeHUD counts households that spend more than 30% of income on housing as cost burdened. Compare Paths checks this after college.

State and nonprofit loans

Colorado has no state student loan program; CollegeInvest stopped making new loans in 2010 and now runs the state’s 529 savings plan (HB10-1428). Colorado’s state grant, the Colorado Student Grant, is need-based and goes only to students at Colorado colleges. Some other states’ nonprofit lenders lend to families nationwide. For 2026–27, Massachusetts’ MEFA offers fixed rates from 4.95% to 8.90% depending on credit, compared with 9.07% plus a 4.228% fee for Parent PLUS (MEFA). Loans like these are approved on credit and don’t come with the federal options, such as income-based repayment and federal deferment and discharge rules. Most aid experts suggest using the student’s federal loans first, then comparing Parent PLUS with state, nonprofit and private loans on rate, fees and repayment terms.

Questions to ask every financial aid office

  1. Is this offer for 2027–28, or does it use last year’s costs?
  2. Which grants renew each year, and what GPA or credits keep them?
  3. If my student wins an outside scholarship, do you lower loans first or your own grants?
  4. Are books, travel and personal costs included in the cost you listed?
  5. Can we appeal this offer if our finances changed, or if another college offered more?
  6. When is the first bill due, and is there a monthly payment plan?

Talk it through together

Money is one part of a bigger question: where will your student grow? A few questions, asked before offers arrive in the spring, can turn a stressful decision into a shared one.

  • What can our family pay each year without new debt? Does our student know that number?
  • How much, if anything, is our student willing to borrow, and what monthly payment would that mean after graduation?
  • What matters most to our student in a college, and what is it worth to us?
  • If two colleges both fit, how will we decide between them?
  • Who else depends on these savings: brothers and sisters, or our own retirement?
