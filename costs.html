What college costs, piece by piece

The price on a college’s website is its cost of attendance: everything one year of college is expected to cost, whether the college bills you for it or not. Pick a college to see the pieces, how much living off campus or at home changes the total, and why the same college can cost one family twice what it costs another.

🔒Nothing you type or choose here is saved or sent anywhere. The math runs on your device.

1. Pick a college

Published costs from the federal College Scorecard. Each college’s own website has the newest numbers.

2. What each word on the price means

Tuition and fees

What the college charges for classes. Required fees (technology, health center, recreation, student activities) are added on, and the Scorecard numbers here include them. Billed by the college.

Room

Housing. On campus, it’s a dorm room, and the price changes with the hall and the number of roommates. Most colleges require first-year students to live on campus. Billed by the college on campus. Off campus, it’s rent you pay a landlord.

Board (meal plan)

Food. A meal plan is a set number of meals a week (or unlimited “swipes”) plus “dining dollars” for campus cafés. Dorm residents usually must buy one. Ask what happens to unused meals. Billed by the college. Room and board together are the “housing and food” line.

Books and supplies

Textbooks, course materials, a laptop, lab fees some courses add. Not billed: you buy these yourself, so they don’t appear on the bill but still cost money.

Transportation and personal

Flights or gas home, a bus pass, a phone, clothes, laundry, toiletries, a little fun. Not billed. Colleges far from home don’t always count enough for travel; check.

Cost of attendance

All of the above together, for about nine months of school. Financial aid can never be more than this number, so it also sets the most a student can receive in aid and loans.

Billed vs. not billed

The bill from the college shows only tuition, fees, and on-campus housing and meals. Books, travel and personal costs, and off-campus rent and groceries, come out of your pocket during the year, so plan for them as well.

Net price

Cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships: what your family pays from savings, income and loans. It’s the number to compare. Each college’s net price calculator estimates yours.

4. Why the same college costs some families more

In-state, out-of-state, and the Western discount

States pay part of the cost of educating their own residents at their public colleges, so residents pay in-state tuition and everyone else pays out-of-state tuition, often two to four times as much. Housing, food and books cost the same either way. The difference is all in the tuition line.

Colorado’s cap. Each year, the state budget (the “Long Bill”) limits how much Colorado’s public colleges can raise tuition for resident undergraduates. For 2026–27 the cap is 3.5% (5% at community colleges), and the same budget added no new state financial aid. CU Boulder raised tuition 3% for new students and holds that rate for four years. Out-of-state tuition has no cap. CU Board of Regents, April 16, 2026, CU Boulder Today

WUE. Through the Western Undergraduate Exchange, Colorado students can pay at most 150% of in-state tuition at participating public colleges in other western states. In 2025–26 the average WUE student saved $13,060. Each college sets its own WUE rules (GPA, majors, deadlines, number of seats), so check the college’s WUE page. Colorado students also pay in-state tuition at South Dakota’s public universities. WICHE list of WUE schools, WICHE, 2025–26 by the numbers, WICHE Colorado fact sheet

Private colleges: almost no one pays the sticker price

Private colleges set a high published price, then give most students their own grants. For 2025–26, private nonprofit colleges gave back about 57 cents in grants for every dollar of tuition they charged first-year students, and about 9 in 10 first-year students got some. That’s why a private college can cost less than an out-of-state public one, and why the net price calculator matters more than the sticker price. NACUBO Tuition Discounting Study, via Higher Ed Dive

Where these numbers come from
  • College costs: U.S. Department of Education, College Scorecard, latest year (2025–26 costs), pulled November 10, 2026: tuition and required fees (in-state and out-of-state), on-campus room and board, the college’s off-campus housing and food estimate, books and supplies, and other costs on campus, off campus and living with family.
  • Rents: HUD Small Area Fair Market Rents, FY2027 (effective October 1, 2026), for the college’s ZIP code, or the nearest city ZIP code when HUD lists none for a campus ZIP. A fair market rent is HUD’s estimate of a typical, modest rental, including basic utilities.
  • WUE estimate: 150% of the college’s in-state tuition and fees, or its out-of-state rate if lower. The college’s own WUE rate may differ.
  • Planner defaults (internet, insurance, food, transportation, move-in) are starting guesses, not data. Change them.
